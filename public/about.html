Flooding, Climate and the Waterfront

New York & New Jersey · CUNY Graduate Center

A teaching platform for a course on flooding, climate change and the urban waterfront. It puts the city's own maps of its water beside each other and asks which of them a person standing on a given block would recognise.

The exercise this site exists for

New York City has had two simultaneously-operative, mutually inconsistent regulatory floodplains for over a decade. FEMA issued Preliminary FIRMs in December 2013; the version embedded in the Building Code is dated 2015. The City filed a technical appeal. On 17 October 2016 FEMA agreed that its models had overstated flood elevations by one to two and a half feet citywide, and committed to reissue. No revised maps have been adopted.

So today flood insurance rates run off the 2007 Effective FIRMs, while the Building Code requires new and substantially improved buildings to use the 2015 Preliminary FIRMs. Turn both on. Then put the Sandy Inundation Zone — where the water actually went — on top.

Second: map Ida's basement flooding against the FEMA floodplain. There is almost no overlap, because FEMA's product is coastal and Ida was pluvial. That is the finding, not an error. The layer that does capture it is DEP's Stormwater Flood Maps.

What is missing, and why that matters

Several of these gaps were searched for and confirmed absent. They are not search failures; they are the most teachable material here.

  • There is no City dataset of piers, wharves, bulkheads, seawalls, revetments or riprap. For roughly 520 miles of shoreline, the structures holding the edge in place are absent from the municipal commons. Everything usable comes from the navy's chartmakers — the NOAA ENC layers on this map.
  • The 2021 Comprehensive Waterfront Plan shipped no GIS: a 155 MB PDF and six topic PDFs.
  • There is no official geometry for any coastal protection project. Living Breakwaters is a completed $111M structure in the harbour with no published alignment.
  • The Staten Island Sandy buyout footprints — Oakwood Beach, Ocean Breeze, Graham Beach — are not published as state GIS.
  • DEP publishes 98,288 harbour water-quality measurements with no coordinates. A century-old sampling programme whose measurements are public and whose places are not.
  • Neither the MTA nor the Port Authority publishes any flood or resilience geodata.

Licensing, and an inversion worth teaching

  • Federal is clean. NOAA and USACE state no access constraints. USGS 3DEP is "free of charge and without use restrictions".
  • The City states no licence at all on most of these layers — only NYC.gov's general no-warranty terms apply.
  • NYSDEC is the one real constraint. Its layers carry "Secondary Distribution of the data is not allowed" plus a credit requirement. Six layers here are affected. They are built and marked DEC — not re-served: usable in class, held back from public re-serving until DEC's position is in writing.
  • NYSERDA's sea-level-rise data states no licence anywhere.
  • And the lesson: the only explicit licence found across the whole survey is NYC Ferry's, and it is the most restrictive — revocable, non-transferable, non-assignable. The federal navy-chart data is freer than the city's ferry timetable.

Caveats that belong in the seminar, not the footnotes

  • 311 measures reporting, not flooding. Differential propensity to call — by tenure, immigration status, language, and prior experience of non-response — is the classic confound. It is exactly what ethnography can address and the API cannot.
  • The City's published future floodplains (2020s, 2050s, 2080s, 2100) are built on the 2015 Preliminary FIRMs the City successfully appealed, so they overstate depth by 1–2.5 ft. They remain the City's published future floodplains regardless.
  • Vertical datum for flood work here is NAVD88. Do not mix it with MLLW or MHHW without saying so.
  • Socrata's updatedAt is a metadata timestamp. Several flood layers advertise 2026 while the geometry has not changed since 2013. Use rowsUpdatedAt.

What is here, and what is only named

The catalogue holds 106 entries — 191 counting every scenario variant — and every one of them appears in the panel, including the ones this site cannot draw. Each carries a status:

  • live — fetched from the publisher's own service the moment you switch it on, so it is never staler than the agency's map.
  • baked — a local copy, fetched whole, past the 2,000-feature cap a single live query stops at.
  • not yet — real, described, and not yet fetchable in a browser: a zip, a geodatabase, a raster set, a tabular API.
  • DEC — held — NYSDEC forbids secondary distribution, so the six affected layers are named and never served.
  • not published — searched for and confirmed missing from the public record.

Listing the last two is the point. A catalogue that quietly omits what nobody publishes misrepresents the record, and the shape of that record — who maps what, and who declines to — is half of what this course is about.

How it is built

A static site: plain ES modules, MapLibre GL JS 5.24.0, and PMTiles for the large layers, each a single archive served over HTTP range requests. Small layers are fetched live from the publisher, so they are never staler than the agency's own map. Supabase carries course membership under row-level security; Vercel hosts it. Source at the repository linked from the course page.

Coverage: New York and New Jersey, centred on New York City. The Where chips in the panel filter by which shore a layer covers. New Jersey is presently served by the bi-state sources — FEMA's National Flood Hazard Layer, NOAA's sea-level-rise depth rasters and merged polygons, USACE channels and eHydro, the NOAA charts, the National Risk Index and the NFIP tables. A dedicated survey of NJDEP and NJGIN is the next piece of work.
